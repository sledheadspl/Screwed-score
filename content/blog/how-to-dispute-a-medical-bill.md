# How to Dispute a Medical Bill: A Step-by-Step Guide

Medical bills are some of the hardest bills to read and some of the easiest to get wrong. A single mistyped code, a duplicate line, or a claim your insurer never processed correctly can add hundreds or thousands of dollars to what you owe. The good news is that you have real rights here, and most billing offices expect some patients to push back.

This guide walks through what to do, in order, before you pay.

## 1. Don't pay the first bill you get

The first statement from a hospital is usually a summary: a total, a due date and very little detail. Paying it settles nothing about whether it's correct. If you can, hold off while you check it. If the due date is close, call the billing office, tell them you're reviewing the bill, and ask them to note your account so it isn't sent to collections while you do.

## 2. Ask for an itemized bill

Call the billing office and ask for a **fully itemized bill** that lists every charge, with dates of service and billing codes (CPT or HCPCS codes for services and supplies). You're entitled to see what you're being charged for. This is where errors become visible.

While you're at it, ask your insurer for the **Explanation of Benefits (EOB)** for that visit. The EOB shows what the provider billed, what your plan allowed, what it paid and what it says you owe. Your bill and your EOB should match. If they don't, that's your first question.

## 3. Check the bill line by line

Look for these common problems:

- **Duplicate charges.** The same service, test or medication listed twice on the same date.
- **Services you didn't receive.** A test that was ordered and then cancelled, or a procedure that never happened.
- **Wrong dates.** Charges for days you weren't there, or an extra room-and-board day after you were discharged.
- **Upcoding.** A service billed at a more complex (and more expensive) level than what was actually done.
- **Unbundling.** Several separate charges for things that are normally billed together as one.
- **Wrong patient or insurance details.** A typo in your member ID can cause a denial that then lands on you as "patient responsibility".
- **Out-of-network surprises.** A provider you didn't choose (an anesthesiologist or radiologist, for example) billing you at out-of-network rates for care at an in-network hospital.

You don't need to know every code. Look up any code you don't recognize, and mark anything that doesn't match what you remember happening.

## 4. Know your protections

**The No Surprises Act.** Since 2022, federal law protects most people with private insurance from many surprise out-of-network bills, including most emergency care and certain care from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities. In those cases you generally should only owe your normal in-network cost-sharing. If you get a bill like this, say so in writing and reference the No Surprises Act.

**Good Faith Estimates if you're uninsured or self-pay.** If you don't use insurance, providers generally must give you a good faith estimate of costs ahead of scheduled care. If your final bill is substantially higher than that estimate (the federal threshold is $400 or more above it), you may be able to use the federal patient-provider dispute process. You typically need to start within 120 days of receiving the bill.

**Insurance appeals.** If your insurer denied a claim, you have the right to an internal appeal, and if that fails, usually an external review by an independent reviewer. Your denial letter tells you the deadline. Don't miss it.

**Financial assistance.** Nonprofit hospitals are required to have financial assistance (charity care) policies. If your income qualifies, you may be able to get the bill reduced or forgiven, sometimes even after you've been billed. Ask for the hospital's financial assistance application.

## 5. Dispute it in writing

Call first if you like, but always follow up in writing. A short, clear letter or email works best:

- Your name, account number and date of service
- Each charge you're disputing, with the line, code and amount
- Why you're disputing it (duplicate, not received, should be in-network, and so on)
- What you want: a corrected bill, a reprocessed claim or a removed charge
- A request that the account be put on hold while it's reviewed

Keep a log of every call: the date, who you spoke to and what they said.

## 6. Negotiate what's left

Even after errors are fixed, the remaining balance is often negotiable. It's reasonable to ask:

- Is there a discount for paying in full now?
- Do you offer an interest-free payment plan?
- Do I qualify for financial assistance?

Get any agreement in writing before you pay.

## 7. Protect your credit

The three major credit bureaus no longer include medical collections under $500 on credit reports, and paid medical collections are removed. If a medical bill you're disputing is sent to collections anyway, you can dispute it with the collector and the credit bureaus while the original dispute is open.
