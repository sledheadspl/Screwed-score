# How to Lower Your Internet and Phone Bill (Without Switching Providers)

Internet, cable and phone bills have a way of creeping up. A promotional rate ends, a "network fee" appears, an equipment rental you forgot about keeps renewing. None of it is dramatic on its own, but over a year it adds up. Here's how to find what changed and get it back down, usually with one phone call.

## Step 1: Put two bills side by side

Pull up your current bill and one from 12 months ago, or from when you first signed up. Compare them line by line. You're looking for:

- **An expired promotional rate.** This is the most common reason a bill jumps. The promo date is usually printed somewhere on the bill or in your account.
- **New fees.** Look for anything called a network, broadcast, regional sports, "internet infrastructure" or administrative fee. Some are passed-through costs, but many are simply part of the price that's been moved out of the advertised rate.
- **Equipment rental.** A monthly charge for a modem, router or TV box.
- **Add-ons you don't use.** Premium channels, device protection, extra lines, a landline you forgot about, or a speed tier you upgraded to once and never needed.
- **Late fees or one-time charges** that were never removed.

## Step 2: Check what you actually need

Before you call, decide what you're willing to keep. Many households pay for an internet speed tier far above what they use. If you mostly stream, browse and video-call, a lower tier is often fine. For phone plans, check your actual monthly data use in your account or phone settings; you may be paying for unlimited data you never come close to.

## Step 3: Stop renting equipment you could own

If you pay a monthly modem or router fee, check whether your provider lets you use your own. Many do, and they publish a list of compatible models. A one-time purchase often pays for itself within a year. Return the rented equipment and keep the receipt.

## Step 4: Make the call

Call the main customer service number and say you're thinking about cancelling. That usually routes you to the retention team, who have more room to offer discounts than the first person you reach.

Be calm, specific and willing to say no:

> "Hi, my bill has gone from $X to $Y since my promotion ended. I've seen [competitor] advertising [speed/plan] for less. I'd rather stay, but I need my bill closer to $X. What can you do?"

Then:

- **Ask for current promotions** for existing customers.
- **Ask them to remove** any fees you didn't agree to and any add-ons you don't want.
- **Ask about autopay or paperless discounts** if you aren't already getting them.
- **If the offer isn't good enough, say thank you and ask what the cancellation process is.** Sometimes the best offer comes at that point.

Before you hang up, ask for the new monthly total including all taxes and fees, how long the new rate lasts, and whether it requires a contract. Write down the rep's name and the date, and ask for a confirmation by email.

## Step 5: Set a reminder

Put a reminder in your calendar a month before the new rate ends. That's when you repeat this call.

## Step 6: If the bill is wrong, dispute it

If you were charged for something you never ordered or after you cancelled, ask for a refund and a written confirmation. If the provider won't fix it, you can file a complaint with the FCC (for phone, internet and TV) or your state's consumer protection office or utility commission. If you paid by credit card, you may also be able to dispute the charge with your card issuer, usually within 60 days of the statement it appeared on.
