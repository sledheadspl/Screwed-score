Front Brake Pads and Rotors

Do your own front brake job safely and save hundreds per axle.

Time: 1.5 to 3 hours (first time)Level: IntermediateSteps: 6

Brakes sound scary, but a front pad and rotor job on most cars is basic bolt-on work. The keys are support, cleanliness, correct torque, and testing before you drive. This guide covers typical front disc brakes on cars, SUVs and light trucks.

Safety first, read before you start

  • Never get under or work on a car held up only by a jack. Use jack stands on solid, level ground, under the vehicle's specified lift points.
  • Chock the wheels that stay on the ground.
  • Don't blow brake dust with compressed air. Wipe it with brake cleaner and a rag and wear gloves and glasses.
  • Don't let the caliper hang by its rubber brake hose.
  • Brake fluid damages paint. Wipe spills immediately.
  • Pump the brake pedal until it's firm before moving the car. The first press after a brake job goes to the floor.
  • Work one side at a time so the other side stays as your reference.
  • If you're not confident, have a qualified mechanic inspect your work before driving. Brakes are a safety system.

Step 1Do you need brakes?

  1. High-pitched squeal while braking: many pads have a metal wear tab that squeals when pads are low.
  2. Grinding: pads are likely down to metal. Stop driving as soon as you can; this damages rotors fast.
  3. Pedal or steering wheel pulsing under braking: usually uneven rotor wear (often called warped rotors).
  4. Look through the wheel spokes at the caliper. Pad material less than about 3 mm (1/8") thick means replace.
  5. Rotors have a minimum thickness stamped on them. Deep grooves, a big lip on the edge, or blue heat spots mean new rotors.

Step 2Lift and remove the wheel

  1. Park on flat concrete, set the parking brake, and chock the rear wheels.
  2. Loosen the lug nuts about a half turn while the wheel is still on the ground.
  3. Jack at the lift point in your owner's manual, then set a jack stand under the frame or pinch weld and lower the car onto it.
  4. Remove the lug nuts and the wheel. Slide the wheel under the car's side as an extra safety catch.

Step 3Remove the caliper, pads, bracket and rotor

  1. Remove the reservoir cap on the brake fluid reservoir (keeps pressure from building when you push the piston back). Check the level: if it's full, remove a little fluid with a clean turkey baster so it won't overflow.
  2. Remove the two caliper slide bolts (usually on the back of the caliper).
  3. Lift the caliper off and hang it with a bungee from the suspension. Don't let it hang on the hose.
  4. Remove the old pads and the clips. Note how the wear indicator is positioned.
  5. Remove the two larger bracket bolts and remove the caliper bracket.
  6. Pull the rotor off. If it's rusted on, remove any small retaining screw and tap the rotor's hat area (not the braking surface) with a mallet.
Caution: This guide covers front brakes. Some rear calipers with built-in parking brakes need the piston rotated in, not pushed. Check your vehicle before doing rears.

Step 4Clean and prep

  1. Wire-brush the hub face where the rotor sits. Rust here causes vibration.
  2. Clean the new rotor with brake cleaner to remove the anti-rust coating.
  3. Wire-brush the bracket where the pad clips sit, then install the new clips.
  4. Pull the slide pins out of the bracket, wipe them, apply a thin coat of brake lubricant, and slide them back. They should move smoothly and freely.
  5. Push the caliper piston back in slowly and evenly with the C-clamp using an old pad against the piston. Watch the fluid reservoir so it doesn't overflow.
Pro tip: Lubricant goes on slide pins, the pad clips' contact points and the backs of the pads where the manufacturer says. Never on the pad friction surface or the rotor face.

Step 5Install and torque

  1. Install the new rotor, then the caliper bracket. Torque the bracket bolts to your vehicle's spec (look it up in service info or a repair manual for your exact car).
  2. Install the new pads in the clips, with the wear indicator in the same spot as the old one.
  3. Lower the caliper over the pads and install the slide bolts. Torque to spec.
  4. Repeat on the other side.
  5. Install the wheels, snug the lug nuts in a star pattern, lower the car, then torque the lug nuts to spec in a star pattern.

Step 6Test and bed in

  1. Pump the pedal until it's firm. It will take several presses.
  2. Top off brake fluid with the type on the reservoir cap. Reinstall the cap.
  3. Test brakes in the driveway at walking speed.
  4. Bed in the pads in an empty area: about 8 to 10 moderate stops from 35 mph down to about 5 mph, without coming to a full stop, then drive several minutes to cool them. Avoid sitting with the brakes held hard while they're hot.
  5. Recheck lug nut torque after about 50 miles.

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeFix
Soft pedalNot pumped up yet, or air in the systemPump firm; if still soft, have the brakes bled
Squeal after the jobMissing clips or lubricant, or glazed padsCheck hardware; bed-in again
Pulls to one sideSticking slide pin or caliperClean and lubricate pins; replace caliper if stuck
VibrationRust on the hub face or uneven lug torqueClean the hub; torque lugs correctly
Pedal goes to floor and staysLeak or a serious problemDo not drive. Get it towed

When to stop and call a pro

  • Brake fluid leaking anywhere
  • The caliper piston won't push back or the caliper is seized
  • ABS warning light or the pedal goes to the floor
  • Rotors that won't come off without heat
  • You're unsure your work is right. Have it inspected before driving.
