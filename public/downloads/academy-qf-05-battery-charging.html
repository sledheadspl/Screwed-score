Battery and Charging System: Test and Replace

Find out if it's your battery, your alternator, or something draining power, and replace a battery the right way.

Time: 20 to 45 minutesLevel: BeginnerSteps: 4

A dead battery might be the battery, a failing alternator, or a hidden drain that empties it overnight. Replacing the wrong part wastes money. With a multimeter and 20 minutes, you'll know which one it is.

Safety first, read before you start

  • Safety glasses and gloves. Battery acid burns skin and eyes.
  • No sparks, smoking or flames near the battery.
  • Remove rings and watches.
  • Disconnect negative first and reconnect it last, so a slipped wrench can't short to the body.
  • Batteries are heavy (30 to 50 lb). Lift with your legs and use the handle if it has one.
  • Keep clear of the fan and belts while testing with the engine running.

Step 1Test the battery

  1. Let the car sit at least an hour (overnight is better) for an accurate reading.
  2. Multimeter on DC volts. Red on positive, black on negative.
  3. 12.6V+ full, 12.4V about 75%, 12.2V about 50%, under 12.0V discharged.
  4. Charge it fully and retest the next day. If it can't hold 12.4V or more after a full charge, or drops below about 9.6V while cranking, it's likely bad.
  5. Most parts stores will load-test a battery free, which is the most reliable check.

Step 2Test the alternator

  1. Start the engine and measure at the battery again.
  2. About 13.5V to 14.7V at idle is typical of a working charging system (check your vehicle's spec; some newer cars vary on purpose).
  3. Turn on headlights, blower on high, and rear defrost. Voltage should stay above about 13V.
  4. Under 13V running means not charging well: check the belt, then connections at the alternator, then the alternator itself.
  5. Over about 15V means overcharging, which can damage the battery and electronics.

Step 3Hunt a parasitic drain (dead every morning)

  1. Everything off, doors closed (latch the hood switch or door switch if needed so lights stay off). Remove the key.
  2. Disconnect the negative cable. Set the meter to 10A DC and connect it in series: one lead on the negative post, one on the cable end.
  3. Wait for the car's modules to go to sleep. This can take 20 to 60 minutes on modern cars.
  4. Many vehicles settle around 50 milliamps (0.05A) or less. Much higher means something is staying on.
  5. Pull fuses one at a time while watching the meter. When the reading drops, that circuit has the drain. Common culprits: glovebox or trunk lights, aftermarket stereos, chargers, and stuck relays.
Caution: Don't try to crank the engine or turn on high-draw items while the meter is in series on the 10A setting. You'll blow the meter's fuse.

Step 4Replace the battery

  1. Match the group size (on the label), terminal position, and cold cranking amps (CCA at or above original).
  2. Note your radio presets and any radio code. A memory saver plugged into the OBD2 port keeps settings.
  3. Disconnect negative, then positive. Remove the hold-down clamp or bracket.
  4. Lift the old battery out and clean the tray and terminal clamps.
  5. Install the new battery in the same orientation and secure the hold-down. A loose battery can short and fail early.
  6. Connect positive, then negative. Tighten firmly and apply anti-corrosion spray.
  7. Return the old battery to the store for the core charge refund and recycling.
Pro tip: Some newer vehicles need the battery 'registered' with a scan tool after replacement so the charging system adjusts. Check your owner's manual or service info.

When to stop and call a pro

  • Battery swollen, leaking or smells like rotten eggs
  • Overcharging above 15V
  • Drain traced to a module you can't identify
  • Vehicles needing battery registration and you don't have a scan tool
  • Battery located under a seat or in the trunk with complex venting you're unsure about
