Replace Spark Plugs (4-Cylinder Engines)

Swap plugs safely on accessible engines and stop a misfire before it costs you a catalytic converter.

Time: 45 to 90 minutesLevel: IntermediateSteps: 4

Worn plugs cause rough idle, hesitation and misfire codes, and a misfire left alone can overheat the catalytic converter. On most 4-cylinder engines the plugs are right on top. This guide covers those; V6 engines often need the intake manifold removed and are a shop job.

A shop charges
$150 to $350
You'll spend
$20 to $60 (plugs), plus a torque wrench if you don't own one

Safety first, read before you start

  • Work on a cold engine only. Aluminum cylinder heads strip easily when warm.
  • Disconnect the battery negative if your manual recommends it.
  • Never pull the coil by its wires. Use the coil's own handle or a puller.
  • Thread every plug in by hand first. Cross-threading a plug can mean a cylinder head repair.

What you'll learn

  • Buy the exact plug and gap your engine needs
  • Remove coils and plugs without damage
  • Torque plugs correctly
  • Read old plugs for clues

What you need

  • Correct plugs (owner's manual or parts lookup)
  • Ratchet, extension and spark plug socket (5/8" or 14 mm, with rubber insert)
  • Torque wrench
  • Compressed air or a shop vac
  • Dielectric grease
  • Gap tool (only if the plug maker says to check gap)

Step 1Prepare

  1. Confirm the plug type and replacement interval in your manual (many modern iridium plugs last 60,000 to 100,000 miles).
  2. Remove the engine cover if fitted. Blow or vacuum dirt out of the plug wells so nothing falls into a cylinder.
  3. Label coils 1 to 4 with tape so they go back in order (they're usually identical, but it helps track progress).

Step 2Remove one plug at a time

  1. Unplug one coil's connector (press the tab), remove its bolt, and pull the coil straight up with a slight twist.
  2. Use the plug socket and extension to loosen the plug counterclockwise. It may be tight at first.
  3. Lift the old plug out and set it on a rag in order so you can read it later.
Caution: Do one cylinder at a time so you never leave a hole open longer than needed.

Step 3Install the new plug

  1. Check gap only if the manufacturer says to. Many iridium plugs come pre-gapped and their tips are easily damaged.
  2. Start the plug by hand with the socket and extension only, no ratchet, for several full turns.
  3. Torque to the plug maker's spec (it's on the box; it varies by thread and seat type).
  4. Put a small dab of dielectric grease inside the coil boot, push the coil down until it seats, refit the bolt and the connector.
Pro tip: Don't add anti-seize unless the plug maker specifies it. Many plugs are plated and anti-seize changes the torque.

Step 4Finish and test

  1. Repeat for each cylinder, reconnect the battery and start the engine. Idle should be smooth.
  2. Clear any old misfire codes and drive. If a misfire code returns, swap that coil to another cylinder to see if the misfire moves with it.

Troubleshooting

If this happensDo this
Plug won't loosenStop. Spray penetrant, let it soak, try again cold. Forcing it can break the plug or threads.
Misfire after the jobA coil connector not clicked or a coil not fully seated.
Old plug oily or white and blisteredOil means a leak into the well or rings; white blistered means running hot or lean. Mention it to a mechanic.

When to stop and call a pro

  • V6/V8 engines where plugs are under the intake manifold
  • A plug starts to turn but feels gritty or loose (threads damaged)
  • A broken plug in the head

Printable checklist

  • Engine cold
  • Right plugs and torque spec
  • Wells blown clean
  • One cylinder at a time
  • Every plug started by hand
  • Torqued to spec
  • Coils seated and clicked
  • Engine idles smooth
