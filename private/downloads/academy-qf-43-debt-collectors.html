Deal With Debt Collectors: Validation and Your Rights

Request validation, stop harassment, and avoid the mistakes that cost people money.

Time: 30 to 60 minutesLevel: BeginnerSteps: 4

Under the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, third-party debt collectors must send you a validation notice, and if you dispute in writing within the validation period (generally 30 days), they must pause collection until they verify the debt. You also control how and when they contact you.

Read this first

  • Never confirm a debt is yours or agree to pay on the first call. Ask for everything in writing.
  • Don't give bank account access or card numbers to someone who called you.
  • Know your state's statute of limitations; in some places a payment can restart the clock on old debt. Get advice before paying very old debt.
  • Never ignore a court summons. Respond by the deadline or you can lose by default.

What you need

  • The collector's letter or validation notice
  • Your own records of the original account

Step 1Read the validation notice

  1. It should list the creditor, amount, and the date your dispute period ends.
  2. Check whether the debt and amount look right.

Step 2Dispute and request verification

  1. Before the deadline, send a written dispute requesting verification and the name and address of the original creditor.
  2. Send by certified mail with return receipt, or through the method in the notice, and keep copies.
  3. The collector must stop collecting until it responds with verification.

Step 3Control contact

  1. You can tell a collector in writing to stop contacting you, or to contact you only by a certain method.
  2. Collectors generally can't call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m., harass you, threaten things they can't do, or discuss your debt with others.

Step 4Spot scams and violations

  1. Scammers pressure for immediate payment by gift card, wire or crypto. Real collectors don't need that.
  2. Look up the collector and verify with the original creditor.
  3. Report violations to the CFPB and your state attorney general.

Troubleshooting

If this happensDo this
They keep calling after you asked them to stopLog each call and file a CFPB complaint; consult a consumer attorney.
You're suedRespond to the court by the deadline; contact legal aid.

Get help if

  • You've been sued
  • The debt is very old or the amount is large
  • Repeated violations (consumer attorneys often take FDCPA cases)

Printable checklist

  • Nothing admitted on the phone
  • Validation notice read
  • Written dispute sent in time
  • Certified mail receipt kept
  • Contact preferences set in writing
  • Violations logged
