Car Won't Start? The 10-Minute Diagnosis

Figure out exactly why your car won't start before you pay for a tow or a diagnostic fee.

Time: 10 to 30 minutesLevel: BeginnerSteps: 5

Most no-start problems come down to one of five sounds or behaviors when you turn the key. Learn to read them and you'll know in minutes if it's the battery, a cable, the starter, fuel, or security, and whether you can fix it in the driveway.

Safety first, read before you start

  • Park on level ground, in Park (or Neutral for a manual), with the parking brake on.
  • Batteries give off hydrogen gas. No smoking, sparks or open flame near the battery.
  • Wear safety glasses and gloves, and take off rings and watches. A wrench across the battery terminals can burn you badly.
  • Keep hands, hair and loose clothing clear of the fan and belts. Electric cooling fans can switch on with the engine off.
  • Never try to jump a battery that is cracked, leaking, swollen, or frozen.
  • Crank for no more than 10 to 15 seconds at a time, and let the starter rest 30 to 60 seconds between tries.

Step 1Read the symptom

Turn the key (or press Start) and note exactly what happens. That sound tells you where to look.

  1. Nothing at all, and the dash lights are dead or very dim: dead battery or a loose or corroded battery connection. Go to Module 2.
  2. Rapid clicking (click-click-click): the battery has enough power to trip the starter relay but not to turn the engine. Weak battery or a poor connection. Go to Module 2.
  3. One loud click and then nothing, while the dash lights stay bright: starter, starter solenoid, or the starter cable. Go to Module 4.
  4. Slow, labored cranking: weak battery, bad cable, or extreme cold. Go to Module 2.
  5. Normal cranking, but it won't fire: the battery and starter are fine. It's fuel, spark, or the anti-theft system. Go to Module 5.

Step 2Test the battery and connections

  1. Set the multimeter to DC volts (20V range). Red lead on the positive (+) post, black lead on the negative (−) post. Touch the lead posts, not the cable clamps.
  2. Read the resting voltage with everything off: 12.6V or more means fully charged, about 12.4V means roughly 75%, about 12.2V means roughly 50%, and below 12.0V means discharged.
  3. Now have a helper crank the engine while you watch the meter. If it drops below about 9.6V while cranking, the battery is weak even if the resting number looked OK.
  4. Do the wiggle test: grab each cable clamp and try to twist it by hand. If it moves, it's loose. Tighten it with the 10 mm wrench.
  5. Look for white, green or blue crusty buildup. Disconnect negative first, then positive. Scrub the posts and clamps with the terminal brush (or baking soda and water), rinse lightly, and dry. Reconnect positive first, then negative.
  6. Measure from the battery post to its own clamp while cranking. More than about 0.5V between them means a bad connection right there.
Pro tip: A big share of 'dead battery' calls are really a loose or corroded clamp. Clean and tighten before you buy anything.

Step 3Jump-start it correctly

  1. Park the good car close by, without the cars touching. Turn both cars off.
  2. Red clamp on the dead battery's positive (+).
  3. The other red clamp on the good battery's positive (+).
  4. Black clamp on the good battery's negative (−).
  5. Last black clamp on bare, unpainted metal on the dead car's engine or frame, away from the battery. Not on the dead battery's negative post. This keeps the spark away from battery gas.
  6. Start the good car and let it run 2 to 3 minutes, then try the dead car.
  7. Once it starts, remove the clamps in reverse order. Drive at least 20 to 30 minutes, or put the battery on a charger.
  8. If it dies again within a day, test the battery and charging system (see the Battery and Charging lesson).

Step 4One click: check the starter circuit

  1. Confirm the shifter is fully in Park. Try starting in Neutral. A worn neutral safety switch can block cranking in Park.
  2. On a manual transmission, make sure the clutch pedal is all the way to the floor.
  3. Check the big cable at the starter and the engine ground strap for looseness or corrosion.
  4. With a helper holding the key in Start, measure voltage at the starter's small signal wire (often called the 'S' terminal). About 10V or more there with no crank points to a bad starter.
  5. A few firm taps on the starter body with a hammer handle while a helper holds the key in Start can get a worn starter to work one more time. It's a get-home trick, not a fix.
Caution: Starter cables stay live all the time. Disconnect the battery negative before you unbolt anything at the starter.

Step 5Cranks normally but won't start

  1. Security light: a flashing key or lock icon that stays on while cranking means the car doesn't recognize the key. Try your spare key. If the spare works, the first key's chip or the fob battery is the problem.
  2. Fuel: check the gauge first, because gauges can stick. Turn the key to On (not Start) and listen near the fuel tank or rear seat for a 2-second hum. No hum means the fuel pump fuse, relay or pump.
  3. Fuses and relays: find the fuel pump fuse and relay using the fuse box lid diagram. Swap the fuel pump relay with an identical relay (often the horn relay) and try again.
  4. Codes: plug the OBD2 reader in under the dash. Even if the check engine light is off, stored codes can point to a crank or cam sensor or a fuel problem.
  5. Flooded engine (fuel smell, after several short start attempts): on most fuel-injected cars, holding the gas pedal to the floor while cranking shuts the injectors off to clear it. Check your owner's manual to confirm.

When to stop and call a pro

  • Raw fuel smell or fuel dripping
  • Smoke or a burning smell from wiring
  • A swollen, cracked or leaking battery
  • Cranking that sounds unusually fast and even, which can mean a broken timing belt
  • A key or immobilizer that needs programming
