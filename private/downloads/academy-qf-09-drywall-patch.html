Patch Drywall Holes So You Can't See Them

From nail holes to doorknob holes, patch, sand and texture so the repair disappears.

Time: 1 hour of work over 1 to 2 days (drying)Level: BeginnerSteps: 5

Patching drywall is mostly about thin coats, wide feathering, and patience between coats. The hole size decides the method. Do it right and nobody will ever know there was a hole there.

Safety first, read before you start

  • Before cutting into a wall, check for wires and pipes. Avoid cutting near outlets and switches, and in the wall behind sinks or toilets. A stud finder with AC detection helps.
  • Wear a dust mask and glasses when sanding.
  • Turn off the breaker if you're cutting near an outlet or switch.
  • Lead paint can be present in homes built before 1978. Test before sanding old paint.

Step 1Pick the method by hole size

  1. Nail and screw holes: spackle, smoothed flush with a 2" knife. Sand lightly once dry.
  2. Up to about 4": self-adhesive mesh patch plus 2 to 3 coats of compound.
  3. 4" to about 8": a backed patch: cut the hole square, screw wood strips inside behind the edges, and screw a cut drywall piece to them.
  4. Bigger than that: cut back to the centers of the nearest studs and screw in a new piece, then tape the seams.

Step 2Prep the hole

  1. Cut away loose paper and crumbled gypsum with a utility knife. Edges should be solid.
  2. For a backed patch, draw a square around the hole and cut it out with the jab saw.
  3. Slide 1x2 or 1x3 strips inside the hole behind the top and bottom edges and fasten through the wall with drywall screws.
  4. Cut a drywall piece to fit and screw it to the strips. The screw heads should sit just below the surface without tearing the paper.

Step 3Coat 1: fill and embed

  1. Mix compound smooth, like thick cake frosting.
  2. For mesh: stick the patch centered over the hole. For backed patches: put mesh tape over every seam.
  3. Spread a thin coat with the 6" knife, pressing it through the mesh. Hold the knife at a low angle and scrape it flat.
  4. Don't try to fill everything in one coat. Thick coats crack and shrink.
  5. Let it dry fully (often overnight with all-purpose compound; setting-type compound sets in the time on the bag).
Pro tip: Setting-type compound (sold by set time, such as 20 or 45 minutes) lets you do several coats in one day. Use it for first coats, then lightweight compound for the final coat because it sands easier.

Step 4Coats 2 and 3: feather it out

  1. Knock off any ridges with the knife before recoating.
  2. Coat 2: use the 10" or 12" knife and spread wider than the first coat, about 2" to 3" past it on every side. Thin at the edges.
  3. Let it dry. Coat 3: a very thin skim coat even wider. This is what makes the patch disappear.
  4. Feathering wide is the secret. A small, thick patch shows as a bump. A wide, thin patch is invisible.

Step 5Sand, texture, prime and paint

  1. Sand lightly with a fine sponge. Shine a light at a low angle across the wall to spot ridges and low spots.
  2. Don't sand through to the mesh or the paper. If you do, add another thin coat.
  3. Wipe off dust with a barely damp cloth.
  4. Match texture: practice spray texture on cardboard first. For knockdown, spray, wait a few minutes, then flatten lightly with a wide knife.
  5. Prime the patched area. Compound soaks up paint differently, and skipping primer leaves a dull spot (called flashing).
  6. Paint. For an invisible repair, repaint the whole wall section from corner to corner.

When to stop and call a pro

  • Water damage, mold or soft, stained drywall (find and fix the water source first)
  • Holes in ceilings over a large area
  • Cracks that keep coming back, which can mean structural movement
  • Suspected lead paint or asbestos-containing texture in older homes
